Overview

A sector shaped by
scarcity

Farming in Saudi Arabia is not easy. The climate is arid and the land is mostly desert, which causes very little natural water. Agriculture creates jobs, supports food security and contributes to the economy.

There are no permanent rivers, no lakes that refill with the seasons. Most of the water comes from ancient underground reserves. These are fossil aquifers that built up thousands of years ago. They have been getting drawn down ever since and they do not replenish on any timescale that matters.

Agriculture's share of the economy has shrunk in recent years but it has not been abandoned. Vision 2030 keeps it on the agenda, partly because food security is a real concern for a country this dependent on oil revenue and food imports.

"Using resources efficiently and promoting productive practices of agriculture, less dependent on scarce water, can help ensure long-term food security."

Water Supply

Where the water
comes from

Most of the country's water comes from underground. That might sound fine until you realise those reserves took thousands of years to accumulate and we are using them far faster than they can recover. Here is how the supply currently breaks down.

62% Groundwater

The country's main water source. These underground reserves took millennia to form and by 2004 had already dropped by up to 60% since 1979. They are not going to refill.

22% Desalinated Water

Saudi Arabia is now one of the world's biggest producers of desalinated seawater. It is costly and energy intensive but the government sees it as the most realistic long-term answer.

16% Other Sources

Treated wastewater and surface water make up the rest. The southwest gets the most rain in the country but even there it is nowhere near enough to support farming at scale.

Historical Context

A century of policy shifts

1970s
Irrigation Expansion

The 1970s saw a push to expand farming across the country. Most of the water needed came from underground reserves and no one was worried yet about what that meant long term.

Late 20th Century
Wheat Incentive Schemes

To chase self-sufficiency in wheat, the government started buying it from farmers at three times the going rate. Production increased. So did the groundwater use.

Early 1990s
Oversupply & Cutbacks

The scheme worked too well. Oversupply drove prices down from around 3,500 to 1,000 riyals per tonne and farmers stopped producing due to lower profits. Saudi wheat production collapsed and the country leaned harder on imports.

1990s
Centre Pivot Irrigation

Through the 1990s, Saudi Arabia invested in centre pivot irrigation, which is a huge circular sprinkler systems visible from the air. By the early 2000s two thirds of farmland was using them.

2005
Aquifer Crisis Recognised

A government report explained aquifer levels had fallen by up to 60% in roughly 25 years.

2016–Present
Vision 2030 & Diversification

Vision 2030 marked a real shift in how agriculture is planned. It caused less open field farming, more greenhouses and hydroponics. In addition, less fossil water, and more desalination. The goal is food production the country is able to sustain.

Modern Agriculture

Greenhouse
cultivation

797K Thousand tons of vegetables
▲ 10.6% growth vs 2023

Tomatoes are the most produced crop, followed by cucumbers. Greenhouses cover 7.8 thousand hectares of cultivated land, cutting water loss and easing pressure on already used reserves.

Greenhouses work better here than open fields. They cut water loss dramatically and let farmers grow crops in a controlled environment without relying on soil or rainfall. The government has been pushing for more of them and the numbers show it is working.

Water for those greenhouses often comes from desalination. Saudi Arabia produces about 20% of the world's desalinated water, enough to keep farms running year round despite a climate that offers almost nothing else to work with.

It is not a perfect solution. Greenhouses eat a lot of energy and desalination produces significant carbon emissions. But compared to draining ancient aquifers to irrigate open desert both are considerably less destructive in the long run.

Economic Diversification

The Vision 2030 agenda

Oil money has kept Saudi Arabia running for decades. It made up nearly 68% of government revenue in 2019. Vision 2030 is the country's attempt to not depend on it as much. That figure had already dropped to 49.4% by 2025.

Reducing Oil Dependency

Oil makes up the majority of the country's natural resources valued somewhere around $34.4 trillion. The longer it stays the main income source, the more vulnerable the economy is to wherever oil prices go next.

Artificial Intelligence

The Public Investment Fund is putting a lot of money into AI infrastructure like data centres, large language models and compute capacity. The bet is that Saudi Arabia can become a tech hub rather than just an energy exporter.

Tourism

Tourism has grown quickly from 3% of GDP in 2019 to 5% by 2024. The Government's target is 10% by 2030. Projects like NEOM and the Red Sea development are providing opportunities for tourists.

AgriTech & Clean Energy

There is real investment going into agricultural technology like precision farming, hydroponics and smarter irrigation. The aim is to grow more food while using less of the water.

Public Services

Health, education and infrastructure are important — they are the basics of what a modern economy needs. Saudi Arabia is spending a lot on public services.
